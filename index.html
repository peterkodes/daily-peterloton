What is the rainbow jersey anyways?

Who, aside from the most fanatics, heard of McNulty? No disrespect intended, quite the contrary! Yet, a week before the World Championships in Canada, if you asked most people that follow, but don’t study road cycling who the top cyclist on UAE team were, you would probably get Pogacar, obviously, DelToro, Almeida, Yates and then maybe McNulty. Even the US team at the World Championships had Simmons and Jorgensen with more visibility. Yet, because it’s a one-day race, McNulty will wear the rainbow stripes for an entire season, all the races he takes part in. Talk about a multiplying effect.

Again, McNulty’s win was spectacular, with all the circumstances favoring him. Since then, there were a series of Italian fall classics, 4 or 5 regional races that attract all level of cyclists. McNulty won the very last one. Can we expect the rainbow jersey to be upfront and highly visible at the top of the races, like it was with Pogacar, Remco, Alaphilippe and Van der Poel, or will it be invisible as a domestique jersey, at the back of the peloton, after McNulty is spent out from pulling DelToro or Pogacar.

Which is why the UCI should reconsider how the jersey is awarded. Yes! Have an annual World Championships, have a winner with all the honors, even with a champions jersey, to be worn the next year. But make the rainbow special, award to the top-ranking rider. Is Pogacar the top ranked rider in the world? Let him use it, so audiences know who the man to beat is. What if the number one spot changes hands during the year? I would change the owner of the jersey. It would be one more step into making cycling a spectator sport, giving full visibility of who the man to beat is, and giving further incentive for the top riders to take part in points awarding competitions.

Peter, editor · N5 · Newsroom

Editor's note: Cycling is not the same for everyone. Take the great cadence debate: what is the ideal RPM, and how do you make climbing easier? It's lovely to watch it play out on YouTube. If you believe one video, 90 RPM is ideal, and on a climb you just shift to an easier gear and keep spinning at 90. Easy, right? Right? Well, the very next week another video defends much lower cadences as the norm, pointing to pre-2000 cycling as proof. Then a third says to pedal at whatever feels comfortable. That last one is my favorite. I mean, if 90 is science, I'll train until I'm comfortable at 90! Dylan Johnson has a channel I love to watch. It's built on scientific papers and his reading of them, and he is the first to admit that some things work for some people and not for others. Then there is the Roberta. Officially it's Av. Cid Vieira de Souza, but everyone knows it as the Roberta, a neighborhood climb: a little under 2 km at an average of 9%. It's constant, with a few ramps up to 14%. Nothing for a pro, and even some of my neighbors would shrug at it. For me, it's a challenge. If I'm well fed, rested and in the mood, I can, with luck, hold 60 RPM. On a 34 chainring! Leonard Lee's channel is one I recommend to amateur cyclists: he's fun, witty and never says no to a challenge. He does say 90 RPM is the norm, though. I doubt he climbed Sa Calobra at 90, and that's almost 10 km at 7%. I bet he wouldn't take the Roberta at 90 either. My take is that the people saying you have to find your cadence and feel comfortable with it in different circumstances are probably right. And I think Mr. Lee would agree with me. Peter, editor

Editor's note: Here are my arguments why cycling is the best activity after you are 50. The most obvious is getting your exercise in. Even at low cadence, your heart rate reached low end zone 2 levels which help maintain weight and body functions. Obviously, you should not exert yourself unless you have a doctors blessing. Then there is the low impact factor. There is very little damage to your body, especially if you do the correct motions of getting a bike fit for whatever bike you are looking to use. Then there is the hobby aspect. You can really dive into the rabbit hole, upgrade your bike, search for components, become an informal, impromptu bike mechanic. Read up on safety, on better riding. If you know what MIPs is, you are on the right track. If you are calculating adequate gear ratios, you are nerding out. If you are using tire pressure sites, like Silca’s to optimize your pressure and even bought an electronic pump to hit the exact pressure, you are way, way down said rabbit hole. The only thing I don’t recommend is to become overtly fixated on speed and on the bikes weight. Regularity yes, getting the most out of your bike, sure! Trying to save 50g by using a different thingy, no. Safety first, regularity second, nice new things third, but not fixation on aero this, aero that (aero socks is not a thing for a regular rider). You can even buy new clothing at every opportunity if that makes you feel better, but always bear in mind, you should be in it for the long run, the consistency, the health benefits. All the rest is icing on the cake. Peter, editor
